Case study · EV Charging · 2024

Nationwide EV Charging Platform

Senior Software Engineer · OONE Power Technology · OCPP 1.6 / 2.0.1 · OCPI 2.2

The problem

Operating EV chargers at country scale is a distributed-systems problem wearing an energy-industry coat. Charge points from different hardware vendors have to connect to one central system, stay connected over unreliable networks, meter energy accurately, take payments, and show drivers a live map of what’s available — while roaming agreements let drivers from partner networks charge on yours, and vice versa.

That means speaking two open protocols correctly: OCPP (charge point ↔ central system) and OCPI (network ↔ network), on top of a backend that treats a charging session — which can last hours and survive network drops — as a first-class, long-lived entity.

What I built

  • OCPP-compliant Central System (CSMS). Persistent WebSocket connections to every charge point, supporting both OCPP 1.6 and 2.0.1 — boot, heartbeat, metering, remote start/stop, and firmware-level operations — with concurrent session orchestration across the network.
  • OCPI 2.2 hub integration. Location, tariff, session, and CDR exchange with a roaming hub, so drivers from partner networks can charge cross-network and settlement stays reconcilable on both sides.
  • Event-driven microservices on Kubernetes. Charge-point telemetry, session lifecycle, billing, and notifications are decoupled through RabbitMQ — a charger flapping on a bad connection can’t back-pressure payment processing.
  • Driver app in React Native (Expo). Station discovery on a live map, session start/stop, in-app payment, and charging history — one codebase for iOS and Android.

Design notes

Sessions are state machines, not requests. A charging session outlives any single connection. The session service owns the lifecycle (reserved → charging → suspended → finished → billed), consumes charge-point events from the queue, and is the only writer for session state — everything else, including the driver app’s live view, is a projection of it.

Protocol edges are kept thin. OCPP and OCPI handlers translate protocol messages to internal events and nothing more. Business rules live behind the queue, which is what made supporting OCPP 1.6 and 2.0.1 side by side tractable — two dialects at the edge, one domain model behind it.

Storage follows the workload. PostgreSQL for transactional/billing data, MongoDB for high-volume telemetry, Redis for hot session state and the live availability the map depends on.

Charge-point counts, session volumes, and uptime figures are operator data — available on request in a call.
